Executive briefing

Finding hidden molecular sensors in the human body

MetaboliteSensorFinder · Maxi (science), Silvia, Richard, Umair (engineering)

Our body has thousands of proteins that nobody has studied. Some of them likely grab useful molecules. We built a way to find out which, faster than any lab could test them one by one.

The problem

More than one in four human genes has no known job.

27.8%
of human protein-coding genes are "dark": little or nothing is known about what they do

5,679 of 20,412 genes (Pharos, NIH, 2026).

Why it matters

An unknown protein is an unused asset.

A protein that grabs a specific molecule can be turned into a sensor that detects that molecule. Finding that match is the first step, and today it is slow and expensive.

Biosensors

Detect a metabolite in blood, food or the environment.

Diagnostics

Reveal changes in metabolism linked to disease.

Biology

Explain what thousands of genes are for.

The gap in scale: up to 7,000 dark genes if the uncharacterized share reaches 35% of 20,000. The measured figure is 5,679.

The idea in plain words

A protein holds a molecule the way a lock holds a key.

Protein
a shaped pocket
+
Molecule
fits the pocket and sticks

When they fit, the molecule is held. That is called binding. Hemoglobin, which carries oxygen in your blood, works this way with its iron-containing ring, called heme.

Question we answer: which dark protein fits which molecule?
The search problem

The search space is millions of possible pairs.

We have 8,685 human protein regions with known binding sites, and 408 molecules to test them against. Testing every pair with detailed 3D modelling is not practical.

Our approach

Three filters, cheapest first.

FILTER 1

Quick screen by sequence

Read each protein's amino-acid sequence and score how likely it is to bind a molecule. Only likely binders move on.

→
FILTER 2

Molecule match

Score each remaining protein against every candidate molecule. Only strong matches move on.

→
FILTER 3

Detailed 3D check

Simulate how the molecule fits inside the protein, only for the final matches.

Every score is recorded, including the ones that fail. Filters decide what gets the next, costlier check, not what gets reported.

Why this design

Cheap checks first, expensive checks only where they count.

  • Most candidates are dropped at the cheapest step, so expensive 3D work runs on a small final set.
  • An AI agent runs the three tools in order, handling file formats and data handoffs that normally take a scientist days.
  • Work runs in batches with saved checkpoints, so a failure does not restart the whole screen.
  • The project works to a fixed compute budget of about $100 in credits, so we measure cost on a small pilot before scaling.
What you get

A ranked, auditable shortlist of protein–molecule matches.

1. Full score table

Every protein and every pair scored, pass or fail.

2. Shortlist

The matches that pass all three filters, each with its stated thresholds.

3. 3D poses

Visual models of how each final match sits in its pocket.

These are computer predictions, not lab results. Each hit needs wet-lab confirmation before any product use.
